Collections

A collection is a public list of songs, like a playlist: Christmas Eve, Communion, hymns for new believers. It has no date and never gets "played", so it keeps growing. This page works out where collections should live and what one looks like, on a phone and a desktop.

Recommendation

Put collections in Songs (direction C), and give each one its own page. A collection is a way into the song library ("what's a good Communion song?"), not a plan for a date. The Setlists page is built around dates (calendar, Next, Then, Played), and an undated list would fight all of that. A fifth tab would crowd the phone nav for something most people browse now and then. If collections turn out to be the main way people use Praise Room, promote them to their own tab (A) later; the collection page itself stays the same.

What worship teams want

What research and worship-leading guides say about how teams find and choose songs, and what each finding means for collections. Sources are at the end.

Songs from people they trust

In a 2022 survey of more than 400 worship leaders, recommendations from friends and fellow leaders, and hearing a song live, swayed choices most. Topping a chart or a new-songs list mostly didn't. [1]

For us
Collections are credited to a team or church and passed on by link between people who know each other. No charts, no trending.

Fewer songs, sung well

Just under half of leaders worry about how many new songs come out. Guides suggest one or two new songs a month and a core list of roughly 30 to 80 songs. [1] [3] [4]

For us
Each team keeps an "Our core songs" collection. A new song shows "Learning" until it's been sung three times, and every song shows when your church last sang it.

A reason for every song

Leaders choose songs to fit the sermon and its Scripture, and favour simple, singable ones over clever ones. [5]

For us
Every song in a collection can carry a short note (when it fits, the Scripture behind it) plus its themes and the key your team sings it in.

Keys a room can sing

A common rule of thumb is to keep the top note around D, between C and E, so most of the congregation can join in.

For us
A collection shows each song in your team's key, and the reader still lets anyone transpose for themselves.

One good chart per song

Open tab sites fill up with near-identical versions of uneven accuracy. Ultimate Guitar now turns away duplicates and asks for corrections to the existing tab instead. [6]

For us
A collection points at the library's one copy of each song. Fixes go to that song, never to a separate copy per collection.

Licensed, and local

A church copyright licence covers using songs in your own services; streaming and wider sharing need more. Cantonese worship songs are a growing repertoire of their own. [7] [8] [9]

For us
Public pages share the song list and notes; whether they show full charts is an open question below. Collections work in 繁體中文 and English.

A library, not a feed

Guardrails so collections never turn into social media. Each is a rule for the build, not a setting someone can switch on later.

We build

  • Search and browse by need: theme, key, language, Scripture. You go looking; nothing comes looking for you.
  • Credit to a team or church, not a personal profile.
  • Private saving: only you see what you saved, and the owner never sees who saved it.
  • Suggest a song: a private note to the collection's editors, who decide.
  • One quiet update: a saved collection that changed is marked in your Saved list. No push.
  • Report a collection to admins, for anything that shouldn't be public.

We never build

  • Counts of people: likes, saves, views, followers, anywhere.
  • A feed, trending, top charts or "popular this week".
  • Profiles with followers, or following people at all.
  • Public comments or replies under a collection.
  • Streaks, badges or "12 churches added this".
  • Ranking by popularity in search, or notifications about other people's activity.

Why so strict: in an experiment with 280 Instagram users, getting more likes than expected made people less lonely but more upset, and visible counts made that worse [2]. Calm-technology practice treats attention as scarce and informs without demanding focus [10]. And leaders already feel there are too many new songs [1]: a ranking or feed would add noise, not help. The only numbers we show are about songs (how many, when last sung), never about people.

Before we build it

Decisions that change the design, roughly in order of how much.

  1. Who can make a public collection? Anyone signed in, or only editors and admins (like writing songs)? If anyone can, we need a way to report or hide one.
  2. Is "public" a link, or a listing? Link only (like public setlists today) matches how leaders actually find songs, through people they know. A browsable directory of every church's collections drifts toward a feed.
  3. Should public pages show full chord charts? Public setlist links show song content today. A collection is longer-lived and wider-shared, so it may need to show the list and notes publicly and the charts only to signed-in teams. Check CCLI's Hong Kong terms first.
  4. Who owns it: a person or a team? A collection made "by Sunday Worship Team" can be edited by the whole team, the way a folder's members can edit its setlists.
  5. Can people save someone else's collection? Saving keeps a live link to it, like following a playlist. The drafts show a Saved section that only you can see; the owner never sees who saved it or how many did.
  6. Who sees "last sung"? Your church's history is useful to your team and nobody else. The drafts show it only to people in the church, never on public pages.
  7. Do collections carry keys? The drafts show each song's own key and let every reader transpose. A team might want "our key" saved instead.
