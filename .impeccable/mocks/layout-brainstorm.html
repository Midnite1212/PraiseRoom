Public setlist view: layout brainstorm

Five ways to lay out the page that a shared setlist link opens. These are structure and flow only; any of them can wear the Liquid Glass styling later. Every concept is checked against the three places the page gets used, and against the two things you said happen mid-song: scrolling and text layout, and moving to the next or previous song.

In hand

One person or a congregation member following along. Portrait phone, thumb reach, one-handed.

On a stand

A musician with both hands on an instrument. Phone or tablet at arm's length, often landscape. Touches must be few and large.

Shared to a TV

A small group reading together from a laptop screen-shared to a TV. Big lyrics, little chrome, one person driving.

Sorted by how often they're touched once the music starts. This holds across every concept below; the concepts differ in where the reading surface goes, not in this ranking.

Five concepts

Each one is shown on the device where it matters most, with the split (left and right) behaviour you asked for to make song transitions easier.

A

Scroll & Dock

Today's mock, tidied. One song scrolls; a dock at the bottom carries previous, the set strip and next. On wide screens a narrow "on deck" column previews the next song's opening on the right.

Split

A preview, not a true split: the next song's first verse sits in a narrow column. Tapping it advances.

Transitions

"Up next" at the end of every song, plus the dock. Scrolling past the end could pull you into the next song.

Strong at

  • Phone in hand; the most familiar pattern
  • Already built, so the least work

Watch out

  • Scrolling with your hands on an instrument
  • Too much chrome for a TV
B

Stage Split Recommended for wide screens

Two songs side by side: the current song on the left, the next one waiting on the right. Advancing slides the right pane over to the left and brings the following song in on the right. On a portrait phone it collapses to one song, with the next one peeking at the edge.

Split

Literally the split you described. Each pane scrolls on its own. The right pane is quieter (dimmer, no key controls) until it becomes the current song.

Transitions

You can read the next song's first line while finishing this one, and one touch (or a pedal press) moves everything along.

Strong at

  • Stands and laptops; the smoothest transitions
  • Two songs in view helps a medley

Watch out

  • Half width means more line wrapping at large text sizes
  • A portrait phone can't split, so it needs the peek
C

Page Spreads

No scrolling: lyrics flow into screen-sized pages you turn like a music book, by tapping the left or right edge, swiping, or pressing a pedal. On wide screens you see two pages at once, so at the end of a song the left page finishes it and the right page starts the next.

Split

It happens naturally: the split shows whatever two pages are open, and at a song boundary that's the end of one song and the start of the next.

Transitions

Turning a page is the same action within a song and between songs, so musicians learn one move.

Strong at

  • Stands with a Bluetooth pedal; no scrolling at all
  • TV: whole pages, no half-scrolled lines

Watch out

  • Changing text size reflows the pages
  • Less natural for a phone in hand, where scrolling is expected
  • The most build effort
D

One Long Set

The whole setlist reads as one continuous document. Each song is a section with a sticky header (title and key), so moving to the next song is just reading on. Auto-scroll fits naturally. On wide screens the set flows across columns, so one song's end sits beside the next song's start.

Split

Comes from the columns: the boundary between songs moves through the split as you read, not on a fixed song pairing.

Transitions

None needed; the next song is just the next section. The strip tracks where you are as you scroll.

Strong at

  • Scrolling and auto-scroll, your top mid-song action
  • Simple mental model: the set is one page

Watch out

  • Each song has its own key and layout settings inside one scroll
  • Jumping back to a song means scrolling or using the strip
E

Present (TV mode) Recommended as a mode

A full-screen mode, not a whole page layout, that any concept can switch into for screen-sharing. Lyrics only, very large, one section (verse or chorus) at a time, with the next line previewed at the bottom. Arrow keys, space or a clicker move through sections and then songs.

Split

Not used here. On a TV, one big section at a time reads better than two songs.

Transitions

The last section of a song is followed by a title card for the next song, then its first section.

Strong at

  • Small groups reading together on a TV
  • Readable from across a room

Watch out

  • Not for musicians who need the whole chord chart
  • Needs songs to be split into sections, which they already are

Side by side

More squares is better. Build effort counts down, so more squares means less work.

New UX ideas

Additions worth considering, whichever layout wins. Each says where it fits best.

Keep the screen awake

While a setlist is open, stop the phone or tablet from dimming and locking mid-song. One browser call (the Wake Lock API), and a must for stands.

All concepts · small effort
Pedal & keyboard control

Bluetooth page-turner pedals send arrow and page keys. Map ← → to songs and PgUp/PgDn/Space to scrolling or pages, so a musician never touches the screen.

B, C, E · small effort
Swipe between songs

A horizontal swipe on the reading area changes song on phones, with the next song peeking in at the edge.

A, B · small effort
Auto-scroll

A play button with a speed setting that scrolls at a steady pace and pauses on touch. Speed is remembered per song on this device.

A, B, D · medium effort
Jump to a section

Verse / Chorus / Bridge chips on the song header jump straight there; this is useful when the leader calls "back to the chorus".

All concepts · small effort
Orientation-aware layout

Turning a tablet or phone to landscape switches to the split view automatically; portrait goes back to one song. No setting to find.

B, C · small effort
Fit to screen

One tap sizes the text so the whole song (or the current page) fits the screen without scrolling, where it can.

B, C, E · medium effort
Works offline

Cache the opened setlist so it keeps working when a venue's Wi-Fi drops (a PWA).

All concepts · medium effort
Follow the leader

Later: when the worship leader changes song, everyone's screen follows. This needs a live server channel, so it's a bigger, separate project.

Future · large effort

Decided so far

  1. Portrait phone → A (Scroll & Dock), with no peek. The next song's opening flows on after the end of the current one (option 3), the dock's next button names the next song (option 1), and swipe stays as a hidden gesture (option 5).
  2. Landscape tablet or laptop → B (Stage Split), switched on by orientation and width. The right pane is the next song by default, but people can pick any song for either pane and reorder the set for themselves. Advancing slides the panes: the right song moves left and becomes current.
  3. Shared to a TV → E (Present), lyrics only by default, with a Show chords toggle.
  4. Quick wins for every layout: keep the screen awake, pedal and keyboard control, section jump, and auto-scroll.

Split: picking and reordering songs

The public link is signed out and shared, so picking and reordering change your view only, remembered on this device; the leader's set is untouched. "Follow the set" puts everything back.

Picking

Each pane's title is a button that opens the song list. Choosing a song pins it; "Next song" unpins the right pane so it follows the set again.

Reordering

Drag handles in the song list (an Edit order mode). The strip, the dock and "next" all follow your order. A "Your order" chip shows the order differs from the leader's.

Advancing with a pin

If the right pane is pinned, advancing slides it over to the left and the right pane goes back to following the set.

Watch out

  • People may forget they reordered, so keep the chip and Reset visible
  • Reordering on a phone needs an explicit mode so swipes don't drag rows

Portrait phone: reaching the next song without a peek

Five options. On a stand, the next song's opening matters as much as the button, so I'd combine one that shows it with one that makes advancing a single touch.

1 · The dock names it

The next button grows into "Be Thou My Vision ›" as you reach the last section. One tap, always in thumb reach, and the title tells you what's coming.

2 · Pull past the end

Keep scrolling past the last line and a "Release for…" card appears. Natural when you're already scrolling, but easy to trigger by accident on a stand.

3 · Flow on

The end of the song is followed by the next song's title and its first lines, dimmed. Scrolling into it makes it current. This is the portrait version of the split: you see what's next while finishing.

4 · Next-up sheet

A slim bar above the dock shows the next song's first line; drag it up for a half-height preview. A top/bottom split on demand, but it costs reading space.

5 · Swipe, no peek

A horizontal swipe changes song with a slide; nothing on screen hints at it except the strip. Clean, but hidden, so it suits people who already know it.

Decided

3 + 1: Flow on shows the next opening (the split's benefit, in portrait), and the dock's named next button makes advancing one tap. Keep 5 as a gesture for people who know it.
